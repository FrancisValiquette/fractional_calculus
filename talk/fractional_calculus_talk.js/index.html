Introduction to Fractional Calculus

Goal


In Calculus I and II $$ \cdots \xleftarrow{D^{-1}} \iint f \xleftarrow{D^{-1}} \int f \xleftarrow{D^{-1}} f \overset{D}{\longrightarrow} f^\prime \overset{D}{\longrightarrow} f^{\prime\prime} \overset{D}{\longrightarrow} \cdots $$ effectively learning ``integer Calculus" $$ D^n[f]\qquad \text{where}\qquad n\;\in\; \mathbb{Z} $$ The goal today is to compute $$ D^\alpha[f]\qquad \text{where}\qquad \alpha\;\in\; \mathbb{R} $$

Leibniz, who introduced the notations $$ \frac{df}{dx} \qquad \text{and}\qquad \int f(x)\, dx $$ wrote in September of 1695 to his friend l'Hospital:

Jean Bernoulli seems to have told you of me having mentioned to him a marvelous analogy which makes it possible to say in a way that successive differentials are in geometric progression. One can ask what would be a differential having as its exponent a fraction. You see that the result can be expressed by an infinite series, although this seems removed from Geometry, which does not yet know of such fractional exponents. It appears that one day these paradoxes will yield useful consequences, since there is hardly a paradox without utility. Thoughts that mattered little in themselves may give occasion to more beautiful ones.

The Factorial and Gamma Function

Definition: The factorial of $n\in \mathbb{N}$ is \[ n! = 1\cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n\qquad \text{and}\qquad 0! = 1. \]

Exercise: Compute $4!$

Definition: For $x \in \mathbb{R}^+$, the gamma function is defined by the improper integral \[ \Gamma(x) = \int_0^\infty t^{x-1}e^{-t}\, dt. \] The function is extended to $\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}$ using the identity \[ \Gamma(x+1) = x\, \Gamma(x). \]

Riemmann--Liouville Fractional Integral

Substituting $(n-1)! = \Gamma(n)$ in \[ \frac{1}{(n-1)!} \int_a^x (x-t)^{n-1} f(t)\, dt \] and replacing $n \to \alpha$ we arrive to the following generalization of the notion of integral.

Definition: The Riemann--Liouville fractional integral for $\alpha >0$ is given by \[ {}_aI_x^{\alpha} f(x) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_a^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1} f(t)\, dt. \]

Proposition: The power rule for the Riemann--Liouville fractional integral is \[ {}_0{I}_x^{\alpha}[x^\nu] = \frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(\nu+\alpha+1)} x^{\nu+\alpha}. \]

Practice

Exercise: Using the fact that \[ \Gamma(2) = 1! = 1,\qquad \Gamma(3) = 2! = 2,\qquad \Gamma(5/2) = \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{\pi} \] evaluate ${}_0I_{1/2}[x]$ and ${}_0I_{1/2}[x^{3/2}]$.

It would be tempting to extend the notion of fractional integral by allowing $\alpha$ in \[ {}_aI_x^{\alpha} f(x) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int_a^x(x-t)^{\alpha-1} f(t)\, dt. \] to be negative. Unfortunately, this is not possible as the $\Gamma$ function is undefined at negative integers, as it can be seen from its graph.

Fractional Derivative

To introduce the notion of fractional derivative, we extend the property that differentiation and integration cancel in "integer" calculus: \[ {}_aD^{n}_x \circ {}_aI_x^{m} [f(x)] = {}_aD_x^{n-m}[f(x)] \] when $n,m\in \mathbb{N}$.

Thus, to define, for example, the half derivative we use $$ \begin{aligned} {}_aD_x^{1/2}[f(x)] &= {}_aD_x^1 \circ {}_aI_x^{1/2}[f(x)] \\ &= \frac{d}{dx}\bigg[\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/2)} \int_a^x(x-t)^{-1/2}f(t)\, dt \bigg]. \end{aligned} $$

Exercise: Using the fact that $$ \Gamma(7/2) = \frac{15\sqrt{\pi}}{8}}, $$ compute the half derivative of $x^2$.

Riemann--Liouville Fractional Derivative

Definition: For $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$, the Riemann--Liouville fractional derivative of $f(x)$ is given by \[ {}_aD_x^{\alpha}[f(x)] = \begin{cases} \bigg(\dfrac{d}{dx}\bigg)^{\lceil \alpha \rceil} {}_aI_x^{\lceil \alpha \rceil-\alpha} [f(x)] & \text{if } \alpha > 0 \\\\ \hspace*{1.5cm} f(x) & \text{if } \alpha =0 \\\\ \hspace*{1cm} {}_aI_x^{-\alpha}[f(x)] &\text{if }\alpha < 0 \end{cases} \]

Proposition: The Riemann--Liouville fractional derivative of the monomial $x^\nu$ is \[ {}_0D_x^{\alpha}[x^\nu] = \frac{\Gamma(\nu+1)}{\Gamma(\nu - \alpha + 1)} x^{\nu-\alpha}. \]

Applications of Fractional Calculus

Fractional calculus is used to model systems that have

  • memory effects,
  • history dependence, or
  • long-range spatial interactions


that standard calculus cannot capture accurately as differentiation only depends on the local properties of a function.


It is used, for example, in

  • material physics,
  • control systems and robotics,
  • signal and image processing,
  • Biology and epidemiology,
  • economics and finance.
